# Onion sources — batch 2026-10-27

Every record in `batches/onion.jsonl` cites one actually-opened authoritative page about that exact disease on onion. No record rests on model memory or on a search snippet alone. EPPO codes are "" throughout — none of the opened pages showed one, and none was invented.

## Records and their sources

1. **Downy mildew** (`onion_downy_mildew`, Peronospora destructor)
   - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Onion (Allium cepa)-Downy Mildew"
   - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/onion-allium-cepa-downy-mildew
   - Used: P. destructor, fungus-like microorganism overwintering on infected plants or debris; no resistant varieties; many Allium spp. affected; favoured by cool (<72 °F) humid weather; conidia wind-blown long distances, produced at 43–80 °F (optimum 52–55 °F), none when dry above 55 °F; infection-to-sporulation cycle about 2 weeks; hot dry weather desiccates sporulation with partial recovery, disease returns when cool and moist again; slightly pale spots first on leaves or seed stalks, later light brown or purplish; enlarging spots often girdling leaves, grayish violet furry mold on spot surface; systemically infected plants dwarfed, distorted, pale green; plants often not killed but bulb quality poor and spongy; lesion-weakened seed stems may break, seed shrivels; pathogen-free transplants/sets/seed, 3-year or longer rotation, avoiding poorly drained soils, planting with prevailing winds, spacing/density management, drip or sub-surface irrigation instead of overhead, destroying refuse and cull piles.

2. **Purple blotch** (`onion_purple_blotch`, Alternaria porri)
   - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Onion (Allium cepa)-Purple Blotch"
   - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/onion-allium-cepa-purple-blotch
   - Used: A. porri (syn. Macrosporium porri); overwinters on infected bulbs and debris, can be seedborne in onion; spores dispersed by rain, water, equipment/workers; infection often follows injury by other fungi such as Botrytis or sand/dust in windstorms; older leaves more susceptible; moist warm conditions promote disease; spore germination 64–77 °F with rain or persistent dew, optimum infection 77–81 °F, range 43–93 °F, longer leaf wetness needed at cooler temperatures; small water-soaked leaf lesion developing a white centre, zones as the spot enlarges, turning brown to purplish, red or purple margins encircling purplish centres surrounded by yellowish tissue; moist weather brownish black powdery fungus growth on spot surface; large-spotted leaves turn yellow and blow over; bulbs may decay during/after harvest, first as watery rot around the neck with yellowish to wine-red neck discoloration, tissue turning yellow then wine-red and drying papery; pathogen-free seed, long rotations, reduced leaf wetness via spacing/density/irrigation, destroying cull piles and burying debris, avoiding injury, proper field curing and drying several days before topping, tolerant/resistant varieties.

3. **Neck rot** (`onion_neck_rot`, Botrytis aclada)
   - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Onion (Allium cepa)-Neck Rot"
   - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/onion-allium-cepa-neck-rot
   - Used: B. aclada (= B. allii); overwinters on plant debris in soil, infected bulbs, sclerotia in soil; can be seedborne; seen primarily in storage; severe losses driven by excessive nitrogen delaying maturity, late-season irrigation/rain, inadequate or improper curing, improper storage; infection through neck tissue or bulb wounds, sometimes from symptomless leaf infections, often directly via airborne spores when onions are topped before soft top tissues dry; storage symptoms: soft neck tissue water-soaked, yellow discoloration moving down the neck into scales, bulbs breaking down to a soft mass, gray mold between scales, later small to large black sclerotia forming a solid layer around the neck; allow tops to mature, lift/undercut, cure on the ground 6–10 days in dry weather, longer necks at harvest, minimize bruising/injury, store well-ventilated at 32 °F or slightly higher (higher if humidity cannot be held below 75%), rotation of at least three years.

4. **White rot** (`onion_white_rot`, Sclerotium cepivorum)
   - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Onion (Allium cepa)-White Rot"
   - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/onion-allium-cepa-white-rot
   - Used: fungus S. cepivorum (teleomorph Stromatinia cepivora); hardy sclerotia surviving in soil 20–30 years; only Allium spp. attacked; sclerotia infect from 12 inches below the surface; one sclerotium can infect 20–30 adjacent plants; favoured by cool soils, restricted above 75 °F; once in a field, Allium cropping becomes very difficult; spreads with infected sets/transplants, grazing animals, infested soil movement; leaves decay at the base, turn yellow, wilt, topple over, older leaves collapse first; roots rot, plant pulls up easily; fluffy mycelium on remaining roots and bulb; bulbs may become watery, outer scales crack as they dry and shrink; small sclerotia 0.02 inch (poppy-seed size) in/on affected bulb parts, often around the neck; white rot can continue decaying bulbs in storage if humidity is not kept low; pathogen-free material in pathogen-free soil, washing equipment, digging out infested spots with neighbouring healthy plants and soil, landfill or hot compost disposal.
   - Note: the record uses the page's primary name S. cepivorum and states the teleomorph Stromatinia cepivora in its evidence.

5. **Pink root** (`onion_pink_root`, Phoma terrestris)
   - Source: Colorado State University Extension, "Soil-Borne Diseases of Onion"
   - URL: https://extension.colostate.edu/topic-areas/agriculture/soil-borne-diseases-of-onion-2-940/
   - Used (pink-root section): caused by Phoma terrestris; can reduce stands early but most commonly occurs on roots of nearly mature plants; severely infected leaves may show tip dieback; affected tissues later covered by secondary fungi; stunted plants produce undersized bulbs; dead outer scales may be infected but the pathogen does not infect living scale tissue; most obvious symptom on roots turning pink or yellowish-brown, soon shrivelling and dying, finally persisting as red, purple, or dark brown remnants; new roots become diseased and die. General soil-borne context from the same page: favoured by moderate to high soil temperature, frequent onion cropping, compaction, poor drainage, cultivation wounds, low fertility; pathogens survive years in debris and soil; do not plant onions more than once every five years in a field; small grains, especially barley, highly recommended to improve organic matter and reduce pink root and Fusarium basal rot.
   - Note: pink root and Fusarium basal rot below share this one CSU page, cited by section — the same pattern as the ICARDA guide sections used for date palm on 2026-10-24. The UMN onion diagnosis page (opened, see soft rot below) also names Phoma terrestris for pink root and corroborates the root symptoms.

6. **Fusarium basal rot** (`onion_fusarium_basal_rot`, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cepae)
   - Source: Colorado State University Extension, "Soil-Borne Diseases of Onion"
   - URL: https://extension.colostate.edu/topic-areas/agriculture/soil-borne-diseases-of-onion-2-940/
   - Used (Fusarium basal rot section): caused by F. oxysporum f. sp. cepae; usually scattered plants or localized areas in a field with a history of the problem or planted with contaminated transplants/sets; foliar symptoms unilateral or general wilt and yellow to tan dieback of leaf tips mid to late season; foliage death over several weeks; plants can appear until harvest; fungus enters roots or basal plates through wounds (cultivation pruning, pink root infection, other stress), advances upward into bulb scales; roots may rot off, basal plates gray to brown discoloration, white to pink fungal growth between and on scales, vascular system plugged causing foliage wilt and dieback; progressive bulb decay as secondary bacteria enter and maggots feed within tissues; late-season infection may not show until after storage. Same general management context as pink root above.

7. **Bacterial soft rot** (`onion_bacterial_soft_rot`, Erwinia carotovora pv. carotovora)
   - Source: University of Minnesota Extension, "What's wrong with my plant? Onion: Leaves yellow then wilt" (diagnosis page)
   - URL: http://apps.extension.umn.edu/garden/diagnose/plant/vegetable/onion/leaveswilt.html
   - Used: soft rot named as Erwinia carotovora pv. carotovora; onion leaves become pale and wilt; one or several bulb scales may appear water soaked, gray and soft; entire bulb may be soft and watery; foul smell may be present; common on plants injured by onion maggot or other means; develops in warm wet weather, often at the end of the season.
   - Note: Purdue University Extension bulletin BP-23, "Diagnosis and Control of Onion Diseases" (https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/BP/BP-23-W.html), was also opened and corroborates the storage picture — bacteria entering through neck tissues/wounds, pale yellow watersoaked tissue, watery rot with an unusually bad smell, entry only through insect-feeding or bruising wounds during harvest or packing, spread in storage when moisture develops on bulb surfaces, cool dry storage and careful handling — but it names no bacterial species, so the UMN page (which does) is the cited source and its species name is the record's scientific name.

8. **Smut** (`onion_smut`, Urocystis colchici)
   - Source: High Plains IPM (Schwartz, Gent and Bartolo), "HPIPM: Onion Smut"
   - URL: https://wiki.bugwood.org/HPIPM:Onion_Smut
   - Used: caused by soil-borne fungi Urocystis colchici or U. cepulae, primarily affecting seedlings; attacks only onion family members including onion, leek, green bunching onion; problem on seeded onions in muck soils; susceptibility decreases with tissue age, transplant seedlings rarely attacked; affected bulbs predisposed to other storage infections; pathogens survive in soil, disseminated by soil particles or infested debris; symptoms on cotyledons and young leaves as longitudinal blisters blackish with a silver sheen; seedlings often die before or within six weeks after emergence; susceptibility only during germination and emergence, then plants become resistant; survivors become systemic in embryonic tissue and remain vegetative all season; developing bulbs covered by blackish lesions and predisposed to secondary rots; blister skin splitting releases spores infectious for many years; favoured by cold damp spring weather delaying emergence; 3-year or longer rotation to non-hosts such as small grains and corn; transplants instead of seed in infested soil; prevent soil/debris movement between fields.
   - Note: the record uses U. colchici as its scientific name and states the HPIPM page's dual naming (U. colchici or U. cepulae) in its evidence. The CSU soil-borne page (opened, see pink root above) also names U. colchici for smut and corroborates the streak/blister symptoms, the few-weeks-after-germination window, stunting, midseason death, and ruptured blisters exposing black powdery spore masses.

9. **Iris yellow spot virus** (`onion_iris_yellow_spot_virus`, Iris yellow spot virus)
   - Source: High Plains IPM (Schwartz and Bartolo), "HPIPM: Onion Iris Yellow Spot"
   - URL: https://wiki.bugwood.org/HPIPM:Onion_Iris_Yellow_Spot
   - Used: caused by the tospovirus Iris yellow spot virus (IYSV), recently widespread in the western U.S.; also infects leek, Chinese chive, iris, lisianthus and other ornamentals; reportedly vectored only by onion thrips (Thrips tabaci); acquired by first larval instars, transmitted only after circulation/replication in the vector within 10 minutes of feeding; thrips viruliferous for life, virus not known to pass to thrips progeny through eggs; onion seed, bulbs and roots not known to carry the virus; volunteer onions often symptomatic in early spring in Colorado; likely overwinters in perennial/winter annual weeds, over-wintering onion, adult thrips; symptoms first straw-colored lenticular lesions on leaves, possibly with green centres or alternating green and straw rings, distinct spindle-shaped lesions often on scapes; plants scattered or widespread, highest incidence often on field edges; no known direct effect on bulb quality, but early-infected plants may produce underdeveloped bulbs that store poorly; total yield losses 1–10% or more in Colorado, significant reduction of colossal/jumbo grades in susceptible varieties; high-quality thrips- and IYSV-free transplants, 3-year or longer rotation, eliminating volunteers/culls/weeds, less susceptible varieties, avoiding crop stress; no varieties completely resistant; overhead irrigation can give some thrips/disease suppression; thrips control alone insufficient, thrips insecticide resistance widespread.

10. **Stemphylium leaf blight** (`onion_stemphylium_leaf_blight`, Stemphylium vesicarium)
    - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Onion (Allium cepa)-Stemphylium Leaf Blight and Stalk Rot"
    - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/onion-allium-cepa-stemphylium-leaf-blight-stalk-rot
    - Used: S. vesicarium (syn. Pleospora allii); typically infects onion already damaged from downy mildew, purple blotch, or thrips; promoted by extended leaf wetness, high relative humidity, moderate temperature 60–70 °F; infected plants and debris are spore sources moving on wind and water and transported in infected fields by equipment and people; symptoms initially water soaking and small yellow-to-brown spots, lesions turning dark-brown-to-black, coalescing, leading to blighting of onion leaves; bulb size reduced relative to leaf infection level; rotations of two years or longer, pathogen-free seed, managing density/spacing/irrigation to minimize humidity and leaf wetness, avoiding overly wet locations, promoting good drainage, minimizing plant injury; disease not frequent enough in the Pacific Northwest to warrant an annual spray program, treat as soon as the disease occurs to prevent further spread.
