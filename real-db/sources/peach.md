# Peach sources — batch 2026-10-30

Every record in `batches/peach.jsonl` cites one actually-opened authoritative page about that exact disease on peach. EPPO codes are "" throughout — none of the opened pages showed one, and none was invented. No pesticide product names or dosages are used in the records; chemical control is stated only as following local label / extension guidance.

## Records and their sources

1. **Peach leaf curl** (`peach_leaf_curl`, Taphrina deformans)
   - Source: UC Statewide IPM Program, "Peach Leaf Curl / Peach / Agriculture: Pest Management Guidelines"
   - URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/peach/peach-leaf-curl/
   - Used: spring leaves thickened, curled, red or yellow instead of green; severely affected shoots dying; irregular reddish fruit lesions sometimes; badly diseased leaves falling by early summer; repeated infections debilitating trees and killing branches; white bloom of spores on leaves after fall/winter rains, found on twigs, buds, between bud scales; survival on tree surfaces and buds; favoured by cool wet spring weather; spread by airborne ascospores or splashing blastospores; only young tissue infected; annual management recommended, timing crucial, one dormant application usually sufficient, more in wet years, added delayed-dormancy application in late winter/before bud swell if rainfall is high or the problem is increasing.

2. **Brown rot blossom and twig blight** (`peach_brown_rot_blossom_twig_blight`, Monilinia fructicola)
   - Source: UC Statewide IPM Program, "Brown Rot Blossom and Twig Blight / Peach / Agriculture: Pest Management Guidelines"
   - URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/peach/brown-rot-blossom-and-twig-blight/
   - Used: collapsed blossom spurs and associated leaves forming shoot blight; gum at the base of infected flowers; twig cankers with tan centres and dark margins; grey-brown spore masses on diseased flower parts and twig cankers at high humidity; overwintering on mummified fruit on the ground or in trees; spring apothecia on ground mummies releasing ascospores at bloom as primary inoculum; all flower parts except sepals susceptible but only stamen infection leading to blossom and twig blight; twig lesions apparently not producing spores; bloom sprays reducing blossom/twig blight and thereby ripe fruit rot loss; removal of fruit remaining on trees soon after harvest before mummification; timing at 20–40% bloom and again at 80–100% bloom on susceptible varieties or with heavy rainfall/high susceptibility; rotation across mode-of-action groups.
   - Note: the Clemson peach page (also opened) corroborates Monilinia fructicola as the peach brown rot fungus, but UC IPM is the cited source.

3. **Powdery mildew** (`peach_powdery_mildew`, Sphaerotheca pannosa)
   - Source: UC Statewide IPM Program, "Powdery Mildew / Peach / Agriculture: Pest Management Guidelines"
   - URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/peach/powdery-mildew/
   - Used: terminal shoot leaves covered in powdery white growth, misshapen and puckered; fruit with powdery white spots scarring over as fruit mature; survival as mycelium in bud scales and as cleistothecia; favoured by cool moist nights and warm days; fruit generally susceptible only until pit hardening, later infections possible, certain cultivars more susceptible; apple powdery mildew fungus Podosphaera leucotricha occasionally attacking peach fruit, inoculum produced only in apple orchards; management focused on protecting fruit, routine monitoring, avoiding peaches near highly susceptible apple varieties (Jonathan, Gravenstein, Rome Beauty); up to three applications from bloom until pit hardening or later when necessary, alternating chemistries, early treatments most important.
   - Naming note: PNW Handbooks and Clemson give Podosphaera pannosa (formerly Sphaerotheca pannosa) for the same fungus; the record uses the UC IPM page's name and states the other.

4. **Bacterial canker** (`peach_bacterial_canker`, Pseudomonas syringae pv. syringae)
   - Source: Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Peach (Prunus persica)-Bacterial Canker"
   - URL: https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/peach-prunus-persica-bacterial-canker
   - Used: can be the limiting factor for establishing a peach orchard in the Pacific Northwest; bacteria surviving outside the plant but needing to enter and multiply in the apoplast; injected proteins and small-molecule toxins bypassing host immunity; watery nutrient-rich apoplast environment; ice-nucleus protein increasing frost wounds; most conspicuous symptoms cankers, gum exudation, dieback of girdled branches, dead buds and leaf spots also possible, heavy gumming usually associated with canker formation; cankers typically much longer than broad, possibly girdling limb or trunk, cambium discoloured and necrotic; cankers developing in winter and early spring, bacterial population declining and callus forming around the edge as growth begins in spring; predisposing factors wounds, frost damage, early dormant-season pruning, heading cuts, incorrect soil pH, poor nutrition; key infection avenues natural leaf scars in fall and heading cuts in spring after planting; ring nematodes associated with increased susceptibility; cultural tactics (resistant cultivars/rootstocks, no interplanting with old trees, frost-sheltered sites, spring planting after likely frost, white trunk paint, no late-season fertility, soil nematode testing, optimal soil/nutrition, irrigation off above-ground parts early, weed control); dormant pruning delayed to January/February, summer pruning after harvest in dry weather, heading cuts only in dry weather with no rain expected for a week, complete removal of girdled/killed trees or branches, no regrowth from roots/trunks after major trunk canker removal; copper-based products not working well under favourable conditions, copper-resistant bacteria widespread.

5. **Bacterial spot** (`peach_bacterial_spot`, Xanthomonas arboricola pv. pruni)
   - Source: Iowa State University Extension and Outreach, "Bacterial Spot on Stonefruit Trees"
   - URL: https://hortnews.extension.iastate.edu/bacterial-spot-stonefruit-trees
   - Used: small angular water-soaked leaf spots at the tips, leaf end eventually brown or black, spot centres often falling out surrounded by a reddish margin, severely infected leaves yellowing and falling; fruit pitted or cracked if infected early, shallower mottled appearance if late; bacterial ooze possibly evident under the microscope; culturing and other testing recommended to confirm since symptoms may resemble other diseases; overwintering in shoot cankers, spring ooze spread by windblown rain to leaves, fruit and shoots; fairly resistant peach varieties available as the best control option for peaches; new plantings away from older susceptible plantings; balanced fertilisation, since over- or under-fertilised trees are more vulnerable; Iowa State Plant and Insect Diagnostic Clinic named for confirmation.
   - Note: the Clemson peach page (also opened) describes the same disease with reddish-purple leaf spots with a white centre and crater-like fruit lesions; Iowa State is the cited source.

6. **Peach scab** (`peach_scab`, Venturia carpophila)
   - Source: Clemson University Cooperative Extension, Home and Garden Information Center, "Peach Diseases" (scab section)
   - URL: https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peach-diseases/
   - Used: also known as freckles; small (under one-quarter inch) velvety dark fruit spots and cracks, joining into large dark lesions in severe infection; leaf infection usually not observed; current-year twig infections light brown after 30–70 days, later enlarging and becoming dark reddish-brown the next season; fruit spots only on the outer skin, peeling removing all traces; rain with 65–75 °F optimal for infection; most severe the first year trees bear fruit; site selection avoiding low-lying ground, proper pruning for air circulation and rapid drying; five applications starting at full bloom at 10- to 14-day intervals if chemical control is desired, all chemicals per label directions.
   - Naming note: other extension sources (Texas AgriLife, Mississippi State) name the same fungus Cladosporium carpophilum, the anamorph; the record uses the Clemson page's name Venturia carpophila and states the other.

7. **Shot hole disease** (`peach_shot_hole`, Wilsonomyces carpophilus)
   - Source: UC Statewide IPM Program, "Shot Hole Disease / Peach / Agriculture: Pest Management Guidelines"
   - URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/peach/shot-hole-disease/
   - Used: main symptoms on twigs and buds, fruit lesions possible in wet spring weather; twig symptoms first small purplish black spots turning brown with a light centre and purplish brown margin; tiny dark brown bumps (sporodochia) at the centre of each lesion, easily seen with a hand lens; affected buds with dark brown or black scales, possibly covered with a shiny layer of exuded gum; distinguished on peach by tan twig lesions with dark margins, usually with profuse gumming; fruit and leaf symptoms much like twig lesions, small purplish spots turning light brown in the centre, sporodochia in leaf lesions but not fruit lesions, leaf lesions possibly surrounded by a light green or yellowish zone, brown centre tissue falling out to leave the shot hole; survival on infected twigs and buds, spores produced throughout winter, spread by splashing rain and wind; favoured by prolonged wetness in fall to mid-winter (twig blight), summer rain or sprinkler irrigation encouraging fruit infection, more infection low in the tree where fruit stay wet longer; management primarily by treatments protecting buds and twigs, dormant-treatment efficacy improved by pruning out and destroying infected wood, sprinkler heads angled low to avoid wetting the canopy; history areas sprayed at leaf fall or November 15 to December 1 before winter rains (also helping leaf curl control); spring bloom application if needed, wet-weather check for fruiting structures in leaf lesions, treatment with a product used for both brown rot and shot hole control if present; bacterial-blast-killed buds similar but much blacker with wilted nearby foliage.

8. **Plum pox virus (sharka)** (`peach_plum_pox_virus`, Plum pox virus)
   - Source: University of Illinois Extension, Department of Crop Sciences, "Plum Pox of Stone Fruits" (RPD No. 828)
   - URL: http://extension.cropsciences.illinois.edu/fruitveg/pdfs/828-PlumPoxOfStoneFruit.pdf
   - Used: caused by Plum pox virus, observed in Bulgaria in 1915 and 1918, reported from most Mediterranean regions and Europe, reported in Pennsylvania in 1999, a serious disease of plums, peaches, nectarines and apricot; virus infecting leaves and fruits; yellowing and necrotic browning ring patterns and chlorotic bands on infected tissues; infected leaves and fruits possibly showing no visible symptoms; Potyvirus group, flexuous filamentous single-stranded RNA particles about 750 nm long; four strains M, D, EA, C; detection and identification by molecular methods including ELISA; transmitted by at least 20 aphid species, only 4–6 important vectors, nonpersistent transmission, acquisition in probes as short as 30 seconds, transmission for up to one hour; planting virus-free nursery stock essential; very difficult to eradicate once established; low-infection orchard productivity prolonged by frequent inspection and removal of infected trees; rigorous aphid spraying possibly delaying spread; tolerant cultivars the only effective control where the virus is common.
   - Honest limit: the report does not detail peach fruit alone, but stone-fruit symptoms in general; the record says so in its safety field and does not assert peach-specific fruit details beyond the report.

9. **Gummosis** (`peach_gummosis`, Botryosphaeria dothidea)
   - Source: Clemson University Cooperative Extension, Home and Garden Information Center, "Peach Diseases" (gummosis section)
   - URL: https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peach-diseases/
   - Used: can kill branches or trees; earliest symptoms small blisters on young bark of vigorous trees, usually at lenticels; infection late in the season, possibly apparent in fall or the following spring; some infected areas exuding gummy resin; two- or three-year-old trees often with sunken diseased areas (cankers) on trunk and major branches; large amounts of gummy exudate or gumballs associated with lesions at multiple sites; bark becoming rough and scaly after repeated infections; no practical chemical control available; most severely infected trees water-stressed; deadwood removal during winter pruning and destruction, summer-pruned wood also removed and destroyed; where gummosis is present, scab-control treatments are the preferred treatment.

10. **Phytophthora root and crown rot** (`peach_phytophthora_root_crown_rot`, Phytophthora spp.)
    - Source: Clemson University Cooperative Extension, Home and Garden Information Center, "Peach Diseases" (Phytophthora section)
    - URL: https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peach-diseases/
    - Used: very important stone-fruit diseases; trees often dying within weeks or months of first symptoms, sometimes declining gradually over several growing seasons; caused by fungi in the genus Phytophthora, most severe in poorly drained areas; infected trees with stunted shoot growth, sparse small yellow leaves, small sunburned fruit, shoot and scaffold limb dieback as disease progresses; crown rot as black decayed areas on the root crown or trunk base near the soil line, cankers exuding gummy resin often present; root rot with few feeder roots present, remaining roots often decayed; no chemical control available in the home garden; most important control strategy careful water management — shallow planting, possibly on a 6- to 10-inch raised bed, no overwatering, well-drained sites, improved drainage of the existing location.
