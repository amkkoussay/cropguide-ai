## Records and their sources

1. **Northern corn leaf blight** (`maize_northern_corn_leaf_blight`, Setosphaeria turcica)
   - Source: University of Illinois Extension, Crop Sciences Department, "Northern Corn Leaf Blight"
   - URL: http://extension.cropsciences.illinois.edu/fieldcrops/corn/northern_corn_leaf_blight/
   - Used: gray to green cigar-shaped lesions 3–15 cm, prominent dark sporulation areas in mature lesions, lesion size varying with resistance-gene reactions; pathogen named Setosphaeria turcica, overwintering on corn leaf debris; races grouped by virulence to Ht genes (Race 1 unimportant due to Ht1 in most commercial hybrids, Race 2 may infect hybrids carrying only Ht1); occurrence at or after silking, most prevalent in warm prolonged moisture, significant grain loss if disease begins before silking; favored by 66–80°F and long dew periods; management by resistant hybrids, fungicides possibly warranted early for seed corn (stated generically, no product named).
   - Naming note: the cited page names Setosphaeria turcica, not Exserohilum turcicum; the record uses the cited page's name and states Exserohilum turcicum as the synonym in its evidence, so the two names are not presented as different organisms.

2. **Southern corn leaf blight** (`maize_southern_corn_leaf_blight`, Bipolaris maydis)
   - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Southern Corn Leaf Blight"
   - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/southern-corn-leaf-blight-of-corn
   - Used: B. maydis, two races — Race O normally leaves only, tan somewhat rectangular lesions with reddish-brown margins; Race T attacks leaves, husks, stalks, sheaths, shanks, ears, cobs, rectangular to elliptical lesions ¼–½ by ¼–¾ inch with reddish-brown border possibly surrounded by a chlorotic zone, stalk/leaf infections starting as purple spots developing tan-gray centers, ear infection as gray-black husk lesion extending into kernels as black felt-like mold, seedlings from infected kernels often blighted; resistant hybrids/inbreds available; foliar fungicides labeled (stated generically, no product named).
   - Supplement (also opened): University of Illinois Extension, "Southern Corn Leaf Blight" (http://extension.cropsciences.illinois.edu/fieldcrops/corn/southern_corn_leaf_blight/) — warm 70–90°F and wet conditions, warm humid climate, monoculture and reduced tillage favoring, mid-whorl to maturity timing, Race T no longer considered a threat since the switch from T-cytoplasm male-sterile to normal-cytoplasm corn. The cited source_url stays CPN; the supplement is disclosed here.

3. **Gray leaf spot** (`maize_gray_leaf_spot`, Cercospora zeae-maydis)
   - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Gray Leaf Spot"
   - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/gray-leaf-spot-of-corn
   - Used: C. zeae-maydis, occurring virtually every season; symptoms first on lower leaves 2–3 weeks before tasseling, lesions long (up to 2 inches), narrow, rectangular, light tan later gray, usually vein-delimited but able to join and kill entire leaves; survival in corn residue, worse in corn following corn, wind and splash dispersal; favored by warm 80s°F and humidity above 90% for 12+ hours; severity depending on hybrid susceptibility and environment; resistant hybrids/inbreds available, rotation and tillage reducing survival; foliar fungicides labeled (stated generically, no product named).

4. **Common rust** (`maize_common_rust`, Puccinia sorghi)
   - Source: University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension (CropWatch), "Common Rust"
   - URL: https://cropwatch.unl.edu/plant-disease/corn/common-rust/
   - Used: P. sorghi Schwein., throughout the US corn belt, overwintering in tropical/subtropical locations with a five-spore-stage life cycle on Oxalis alternate hosts, Nebraska occurrence depending on wind dispersal of urediniospores from southern locations (Mexico, Texas) in early to mid June, several races; early lesions small circular to elongate often in clusters, maturing lesions erupting through the epidermis becoming more elongated with a prominent yellow halo, brownish-red oblong pustules characteristic, spores rubbing off on fingers, lesions sparsely spread on both upper and lower surfaces (unlike southern rust), new infections every 7–14 days, black teliospores late in the season with single lesions possibly holding both spore types then only black; favored by cool 60–80°F and high humidity; resistant varieties most cost-effective, cultivation and rotation explicitly NOT affecting development in Nebraska because the pathogen does not survive there.

5. **Southern rust** (`maize_southern_rust`, Puccinia polysora)
   - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Southern Rust"
   - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/southern-rust-of-corn
   - Used: P. polysora, generally tropical but occurring in important US/Canadian corn areas; symptoms similar to common rust but pustules smaller and almost exclusively on the upper leaf surface, usually circular or oval, very numerous and densely scattered, spores orange on eruption aging to chocolate brown to black often forming dark circles around the original pustule, pustules also on the leaf sheath; favored by high humidity and temperatures around 80°F; resistant hybrids/inbreds available, Corn ipmPIPE tracking tool, foliar fungicides labeled (stated generically, no product named).

6. **Common smut** (`maize_common_smut`, Ustilago maydis)
   - Source: University of Minnesota Extension, "Common smut on corn" (reviewed 2018)
   - URL: https://extension.umn.edu/corn-pest-management/common-smut-corn
   - Used: U. maydis, common where corn is grown but not usually causing significant economic losses; galls any time in the season on any above-ground part, young actively growing tissue especially susceptible, galls commonly on ears, leaves, stalk, tassels, initially white to silvery tissue then dark spore masses inside, leaf galls generally small; overwintering in crop debris or soil, viable for several years; most severe when young tissue wounded by hail, wind, or mechanical damage, rainy wet weather possibly enhancing, poor pollination and excessive nitrogen also favoring, dry or pollination-interfering conditions also favoring ear infection; management by resistant hybrids, crop rotation, proper soil fertility. The page states no rotation length and no fungicide efficacy, and the record states neither.

7. **Goss's wilt** (`maize_gosss_wilt`, Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. nebraskensis)
   - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Goss's Wilt"
   - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/gosss-wilt-of-corn
   - Used: C. michiganensis subsp. nebraskensis infecting leaves at any growth stage; long gray-green to black water-soaked lesions with wavy edges, freckle-like streaks distinctive, bacterial exudate droplets oozing from freckles and drying to crystalline deposits, lesions joining to blight much of the leaf, aging lesions fading to tan, systemic infection giving discolored vascular tissue, wet slimy stalk rot, wilting as if drought-stressed; overwintering in infected corn residue, spread by splashing water and very fine airborne particles, seed infectable but not believed an important dissemination means, entry through stomates or hail/blowing-soil/wind wounds; resistant hybrids available, rotation and tillage reducing survival, fungicides explicitly not effective.
   - Supplement (also opened): South Dakota State University Extension, "Goss's Bacterial Blight Beginning to Show Up in Corn" (https://extension.sdstate.edu/gosss-bacterial-blight-beginning-show-corn) — rainy weather, especially with high winds, hail, and sand blasting, favoring infection; hot dry weather slowing it; rotation with broadleaf crops and small grains other than sorghum (a host); tillage burying stalks speeding decomposition. The cited source_url stays CPN; the CPN page itself states no temperature/weather favoring condition, and the record says so in its evidence framing.

8. **Stewart's wilt** (`maize_stewarts_wilt`, Pantoea stewartii)
   - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Stewart's Disease"
   - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/stewarts-disease-of-corn
   - Used: P. stewartii vectored by the corn flea beetle; lesions spreading from flea beetle feeding scars (tiny leaf scratch), initially pale green to yellow streaks later brown as tissue dies, margins usually wavy but generally following leaf veins, stalk infection wilting the entire plant, cut stalks possibly oozing pale yellow bacteria; most hybrids resistant enough that no further management is required, cultural practices and fungicides not effective, systemic insecticides applied to soil or seed able to manage flea beetles (stated generically, no product named).
   - Supplement (also opened): Pacific Northwest Pest Management Handbooks, "Corn (Zea mays) — Stewart's Disease (Stewart's Bacterial Wilt)" (https://pnwhandbooks.org/plantdisease/host-disease/corn-zea-mays-stewarts-disease-stewarts-bacterial-wilt) — warm winters favoring beetle-vector survival and increasing risk, high temperatures enhancing development, other vectors including 12-spotted cucumber beetle and seed corn maggots with the corn flea beetle most important. The cited source_url stays CPN; the CPN page itself states no favoring condition, and the record says so.

9. **Maize dwarf mosaic virus** (`maize_dwarf_mosaic_virus`, Maize dwarf mosaic virus)
   - Source: UC Statewide IPM Program, "Maize Dwarf Mosaic" (Corn Pest Management Guidelines)
   - URL: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/corn/maize-dwarf-mosaic/
   - Used: narrow light green to yellow streaks along veins of leaves, leaf sheaths, husks; mosaic symptoms possibly disappearing as plants grow and temperature rises while young leaves become more yellow; plants possibly stunted with numerous tillers and poor seed set; infected plants possibly predisposed to ear rot and stalk rot; virus including several strains once grouped in the sugarcane mosaic virus complex, principal California corn strain A; transmitted by many aphid species including corn leaf aphid (Rhopalosiphum maidis), greenbug (Schizaphis graminum), green peach aphid (Myzus persicae); principal overwintering reservoir host johnsongrass, most corn outbreaks traceable to nearby johnsongrass; management by resistant hybrids, eliminating johnsongrass within a quarter mile of the corn field, delaying planting until aphid flights are over.
   - Naming note: this is a virus name, not a Latin binomial; the record keeps "Maize dwarf mosaic virus" as the scientific_name value and states this in its safety field rather than forcing a binomial.

10. **Tar spot** (`maize_tar_spot`, Phyllachora maydis)
    - Source: Crop Protection Network, "Tar Spot"
    - URL: https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/tar-spot-of-corn
    - Used: P. maydis, severe yield loss possible on susceptible hybrids when conditions favorable; small raised black spots scattered across upper and lower leaf surfaces, the spots being stromata, hundreds of sausage-shaped asci visible microscopically, severe cases also on husks and leaf sheaths; tan to brown lesions with dark borders around stromata (fisheye lesions), in Latin America associated with Monographella maydis forming the tar spot complex, M. maydis not detected in the United States; diagnostic distinction that rust spores burst through the epidermis and scrape away with a fingernail while tar spots cannot be scraped off; overwintering on infested corn residue on the soil surface, high relative humidity and prolonged leaf wetness thought to favor development, no temperature range stated; residue management, rotation, avoiding susceptible hybrids possibly reducing development and severity, Corn ipmPIPE tracking, some fungicides possibly reducing tar spot with little data on application timing for efficacy/economic response (stated generically, no product named).
