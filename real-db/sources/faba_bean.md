# Faba bean sources — batch 2026-10-28

Every record in `batches/faba_bean.jsonl` cites one actually-opened authoritative page about that exact disease on faba bean. No record rests on model memory or on a search snippet alone. EPPO codes are "" throughout — none of the opened pages showed one, and none was invented.

## Records and their sources

1. **Chocolate spot** (`faba_bean_chocolate_spot`, Botrytis fabae)
   - Source: PGRO, "TU13 - Diseases in Broad & Field Beans" (Online Pulse Agronomy Guide, January 2025)
   - URL: https://www.pgro.org/tu13-diseases-in-broad-field-beans/
   - Used: PGRO lists both Botrytis cinerea and Botrytis fabae for chocolate spot; the record uses B. fabae as the faba-bean-specific name and states both in its evidence. Small circular chocolate-brown spots on lower leaves enlarging and merging into a greyer appearance; spots/flecks on stems and pods; severe infections can cause total defoliation; prolonged wet/humid weather; winter beans particularly vulnerable at high plant populations; crop rotation and seed health crucial; preventive timing at first-pod leaf spotting, second spray 3–4 weeks later if spotting continues upward (stated generically, no product named).
   - Note: the record's safety field (confusion with Cercospora, herbicide/physical leaf damage, and redlegged earth mite damage starting as silvery patches turning red-brown in large irregular areas on seedling lower leaves) comes from GRDC GrowNotes section 9.15 on chocolate spot, in the same PDF opened for records 6–10 below; the cited source_url stays PGRO, and this corroborating section is disclosed here rather than hidden.

2. **Ascochyta blight** (`faba_bean_ascochyta_blight`, Ascochyta fabae)
   - Source: PGRO, "TU13 - Diseases in Broad & Field Beans" (same page as above)
   - URL: https://www.pgro.org/tu13-diseases-in-broad-field-beans/
   - Used: A. fabae = Didymella fabae; primarily seed-borne, certified or healthy tested seed recommended; winter crops near previous-year fields infected in autumn/winter by airborne spores from infected debris; rain-splashed spread to nearby plants; greyish-brown circular to oval lesions, often lighter grey centre; small dark pinprick pycnidia (unlike chocolate spot); lesions on leaves, stems, later pod surface where sunken and darker; blemished seed; stem lesions can break the stem.

3. **Rust** (`faba_bean_rust`, Uromyces viciae-fabae)
   - Source: PGRO, "TU13 - Diseases in Broad & Field Beans" (same page)
   - URL: https://www.pgro.org/tu13-diseases-in-broad-field-beans/
   - Used: more severe in hot dry summers with cool nights and high humidity; small brown pustules on leaf surface surrounded by a yellow halo, growing orange-brown; brown spores released spreading to new leaves; significant yield impact especially in late flowering, less severe once pods have developed.

4. **Downy mildew** (`faba_bean_downy_mildew`, Peronospora viciae)
   - Source: PGRO, "TU13 - Diseases in Broad & Field Beans" (same page)
   - URL: https://www.pgro.org/tu13-diseases-in-broad-field-beans/
   - Used: fungus can persist in soil for many years; often severe in spring and broad beans, rarely treated in winter beans; pale patches on upper leaf surface, greyish-mauve velvety growth on underside; growing points chlorotic and distorted; pod set reduced; thrives in cool humid conditions; most broad bean and several spring field bean varieties susceptible with variation; treatment timing when lesions are present on around 25% of plants and flowering has begun (stated generically, no product named).

5. **Cercospora leaf spot** (`faba_bean_cercospora_leaf_spot`, Cercospora zonata)
   - Source: Field Crop Diseases Victoria, "Cercospora Leaf Spot of Faba Bean"
   - URL: https://extensionaus.com.au/FieldCropDiseasesVic/docs/identification-management-of-field-crop-diseases-in-victoria/faba-beans/cercospora-leaf-spot-of-faba-bean/
   - Used: caused by Cercospora zonata; problems in some years; wet patches with free water on the soil surface; yield effect 5–10% under high disease pressure; mainly leaves, also stems and pods; lesions start on seedling lower leaves early in the season, expand to severe leaf blighting; dark irregular lesions, darker and more irregular than Ascochyta, concentric ring pattern often visible; spreads to upper foliage; severe infection gives extensive defoliation and pod lesions; unlike Ascochyta, no pycnidia produced; soil-borne inoculum and previously infected material; conidia from short white spore clumps; no varietal resistance; break of at least 3 years between faba bean crops, avoid planting near old stubble; early infection increases secondary chocolate spot risk.

6. **Alternaria leaf spot** (`faba_bean_alternaria_leaf_spot`, Alternaria alternata)
   - Source: GRDC GrowNotes, "Faba Bean, Section 9 Diseases" (June 2018), section 9.18
   - URL: https://grdc.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/366234/GrowNote-Faba-South-9-Diseases.pdf
   - Used (section 9.18): dark brown leaf spots, often zoned concentric brown rings with dark margins; confused with chocolate spot or Cercospora, and with Ascochyta but produces no black pycnidial fruiting structures in leaf lesions; weak pathogen of many hosts, infection often follows insect damage or other leaf spots from rust or chocolate spot; develops late in the season as plants mature; thought to survive on crop residues and other hosts; minor late-season disease, minimal incidence in the southern region (2012 survey); control of Alternaria alone not warranted, no fungicide-efficacy data and no registered products on faba bean, sprays for other major pathogens may give control.

7. **Sclerotinia stem rot** (`faba_bean_sclerotinia_stem_rot`, Sclerotinia trifoliorum var. fabae)
   - Source: GRDC GrowNotes (same PDF), section 9.19
   - URL: https://grdc.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/366234/GrowNote-Faba-South-9-Diseases.pdf
   - Used (section 9.19): usually isolated plants rather than patches; young-plant infection near ground level, slimy wet rot into stem and down into roots, plants easily pulled, blackened base with cottony white growth; older plants infected on any stem/leaf/pod part, suddenly wilt and collapse; sclerotia 2–5 mm on plant surface and in stem central cavity, white at first then black; soil survival several years; wide host range including oilseeds; foliar form spread by airborne spores, rapid development in cool wet conditions; damaged tissue infected more easily but uninjured tissue also infected; root form is a slimy wet root rot with sudden wilt and death; rotation with legumes/oilseeds does not break the cycle, cereals are a suitable break; lower seeding rates, wider rows, good weed control for a more open, drier crop; once established, difficult to control.
   - Note: the section title lists S. trifoliorum var. fabae first, plus S. sclerotiorum and S. minor; the record uses the first-named form and states the others in its evidence.

8. **Botrytis grey mould** (`faba_bean_botrytis_grey_mould`, Botrytis cinerea)
   - Source: GRDC GrowNotes (same PDF), section 9.20
   - URL: https://grdc.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/366234/GrowNote-Faba-South-9-Diseases.pdf
   - Used (section 9.20): life cycle and symptoms similar to chocolate spot; flowers especially vulnerable; minor disease in faba bean compared with chocolate spot (Botrytis fabae) in southern Australia, sometimes found together; discoloured seed may be rejected or heavily discounted, grading worth it if seed infection exceeds 5%; significant pathogen of lentil and chickpea, recorded on over 138 genera in 70 families; does not infect cereals or grasses; invades dying and dead tissue; inoculum almost always present; worst in wet seasons with dense canopies.
   - Note: the section names one registered option for this disease; its product name is deliberately NOT repeated in the record, which says only "follow local extension guidance on any registered option".

9. **Aphanomyces root rot** (`faba_bean_aphanomyces_root_rot`, Aphanomyces euteiches f. sp. phaseoli)
   - Source: GRDC GrowNotes (same PDF), section 9.21
   - URL: https://grdc.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/366234/GrowNote-Faba-South-9-Diseases.pdf
   - Used (section 9.21): chlorosis and wilting with root necrosis; roots discoloured, absence of nodulation; diseased plants in clusters within waterlogged areas; confused with other root rots; survives in soil, exacerbated by waterlogging; spreads crop to crop in soil via infected debris or resting spores; predisposes to secondary Pythium, Rhizoctonia and Fusarium infection; common in parts of northern NSW, also detected in Tasmanian bean soils; unlikely a major faba bean pathogen at present; reduced by crop rotation; bean isolates do not infect peas; no cost-effective pre-plant identification options.

10. **Stemphylium blight** (`faba_bean_stemphylium_blight`, Stemphylium botryosum)
    - Source: GRDC GrowNotes (same PDF), section 9.23
    - URL: https://grdc.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0024/366234/GrowNote-Faba-South-9-Diseases.pdf
    - Used (section 9.23): large grey-black necrotic lesions restricted to leaves, often starting from the leaf edge; differs from chocolate spot (small discrete reddish-brown lesions after extended leaf wetness, increasing rapidly to severe leaf necrosis plus stem, flower and pod symptoms); favoured by warm moist conditions, spores spread by wind or rain-splash; occurs on lucerne, clovers, lupin, bean and tomato; carried over on infected debris, seed and soil (stated for lucerne); minor disease of faba bean in Australia though common in lucerne where it can severely defoliate; unusually high reports on faba bean in northern NSW in the mild wet winter of 2016; as a minor disease, no control is required as a rule.
    - Note: a parallel researcher found the NSW DPIRD 2025 faba-bean guide names Stemphylium only as Stemphylium spp. for this disease; the GRDC section cited here explicitly names S. botryosum, so the record follows GRDC and the difference is disclosed here rather than smoothed over.
